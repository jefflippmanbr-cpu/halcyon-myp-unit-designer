# Unfinished Revolutions
**MYP Year 4 · Individuals and Societies · 6 weeks**

## Unit at a Glance
Students investigate whether revolutions ever deliver on their promises of universal rights, tracing a throughline from the French Revolution through the Haitian Revolution to a contemporary uprising students help select. Working as curators, they build a public exhibition — for parents and a visiting museum curator — that puts these three moments in dialogue and answers a single hard question: do new systems of governance ever escape reproducing the inequalities they claimed to overturn? The unit is anchored physically in London, opening at Speaker's Corner and building toward primary-source work at the British Museum.

## MYP Framework
**Key Concept:** Systems
**Related Concepts:** Governance, Perspective
**Global Context:** Fairness and development
**Statement of Inquiry:** Systems of governance born from revolution often reproduce the very inequalities they claim to overturn.

**Inquiry Questions**
- *Factual:* What rights and freedoms did the French, Haitian, and contemporary revolutions each formally promise?
- *Conceptual:* Why do new systems of governance so often reproduce the inequalities they were meant to abolish?
- *Debatable:* Can a revolution ever fully deliver on its promise of universal rights?

**Lines of Inquiry**
1. What did "liberty, equality, fraternity" mean in law versus in practice in revolutionary France?
2. How did enslaved and free people of colour in Saint-Domingue expose the limits of France's universal rights claims?
3. What new systems of governance did Haiti build after independence, and who was still excluded from them?
4. What patterns of promise and exclusion recur in the contemporary uprising?
5. How do primary sources reveal whose voices were recorded — and whose were erased — across all three cases?
6. What would a genuinely inclusive revolutionary settlement have required, in each case?
7. What does the persistence of exclusion across three centuries suggest about the relationship between revolution and power itself?

## ATL Skills
- **Thinking → Critical thinking:** explicitly taught during the Source Bias Tracker (Wk1) and Comparative Governance Chart (Wk3–4), where students must identify perspective and omission in primary documents.
- **Social → Collaboration:** practised in the Wk3 Harkness Discussion, where students must build shared understanding through structured, evidence-based dialogue rather than debate to "win."
- **Communication → Communication:** taught directly through the exhibition label drafting and peer critique process (Wk4), where students learn to write for a public, non-expert audience.

## Summative Assessment
**Task:** Students curate a public exhibition, *Unfinished Revolutions*, comparing the French, Haitian, and a contemporary uprising through the lens of who is included and excluded from revolutionary promises.
**Audience:** Parents and a British Museum curator, invited to a live exhibition evening.
**Public Product:** Physical/digital exhibition with object-based labels, primary source displays, and a curatorial statement.
**Criteria Assessed:**
- **Criterion A: Knowing and understanding** — (i) use of I&S terminology, (ii) demonstrate understanding of subject content through the three case studies.
- **Criterion C: Communicating** — (i) communicate ideas using appropriate styles for the exhibition format, (ii) structure information effectively for a public audience.
- **Criterion D: Thinking critically** — analyse source bias/perspective, construct an evidence-based argument about the persistence of exclusion.

**What excellent looks like:** A top-band exhibition doesn't just describe three revolutions — it makes visitors feel the tension between promise and betrayal. Labels use precise, subject-specific language but remain accessible to a non-specialist visitor. Every claim about exclusion is backed by a specific primary source, and the student can explain, if asked, why that source is reliable or biased. Crucially, the exhibition doesn't offer a tidy answer — it holds the debatable question open, showing genuine intellectual honesty about how much revolutions actually deliver.

## Week-by-Week Sequence
**Week 1 — Promises on Paper**
- Visit Speaker's Corner; observe live contested-rights rhetoric.
- Introduce the Declaration of the Rights of Man; annotate for bias (Source Bias Tracker).
- Formative: Source Bias Tracker.

**Week 2 — France's Blind Spot**
- Curatorial Studio transformation: physically sort French Revolution sources into included/excluded voices.
- Introduce Haiti as the direct challenge to French universalism (*The Black Jacobins* excerpts).
- Formative: Sorting Studio Exit Ticket.

**Week 3 — Haiti's New System**
- Harkness Discussion: Was Haiti's exclusion inevitable?
- Begin Comparative Governance Chart (France vs. Haiti).
- Formative: Harkness Discussion; Comparative Governance Chart begins.

**Week 4 — Testing the Pattern**
- Finalise Comparative Governance Chart.
- Draft exhibition labels; peer critique using exhibition rubric.
- British Museum curator visit: how objects get chosen/excluded from display.
- Formative: Draft Exhibition Label + Peer Critique.

**Week 5 — The Contemporary Test**
- Students pitch their chosen contemporary uprising and argue fit/break from the pattern.
- Whose Fairer? Ledger check-in: stress-test inclusion/exclusion claims against new data.
- Formative: Contemporary Case Pitch; Ledger check-in.

**Week 6 — Opening Night**
- Full Exhibition Dry Run with peer "visitor" feedback.
- Final revisions.
- Public Exhibition Evening — parents and museum curator attend.

## Formative Assessments
1. **Source Bias Tracker** (Wk1) — builds Criterion D source analysis skills.
2. **Sorting Studio Exit Ticket** (Wk2) — rehearses Criterion A understanding through physical categorisation.
3. **Harkness Discussion: Was Haiti's Exclusion Inevitable?** (Wk3) — builds Collaboration ATL and oral argumentation.
4. **Comparative Governance Chart** (Wk3–4) — scaffolds Conceptual inquiry question, rehearses Criterion A.
5. **Draft Exhibition Label + Peer Critique** (Wk4) — rehearses Criterion C, builds Critique & Revision.
6. **Contemporary Case Pitch** (Wk5) — builds Criterion D and student Agency.
7. **Whose Fairer? Ledger Check-in** (Wk4–5) — stress-tests the exhibition argument against new evidence, rehearses Criterion D.
8. **Full Exhibition Dry Run** (Wk6) — final rehearsal of Criterion C before public product.

## Resources
1. *The Black Jacobins* — C.L.R. James (1938) — foundational account of Haiti's revolution as a challenge to French universalism.
2. *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* (1789) — primary source for the promise students test throughout.
3. *Liberty or Death: The French Revolution* — David Andress (2005) — accessible historian's synthesis for context.
4. *Toussaint Louverture and the Haitian Revolution* — BBC documentary segment (2009) — visual entry point.
5. *Revolutions* podcast by Mike Duncan — Haitian Revolution series (2015–16) — narrative depth for independent listening.
6. "Sudan's Uprising Explained" — Al Jazeera (2019) — primary contemporary journalism for the third case study.
7. Robespierre, "On the Principles of Political Morality" (1794) — primary source on governance betraying its promise.
8. UN Human Rights Office archive materials — primary-source comparison for contemporary uprisings.

## Expert & Community Connections
- **British Museum Enlightenment Gallery curator** — outreach angle: request a session on how objects are selected/excluded from display, feeding directly into exhibition design decisions.
- **SOAS-linked Sudanese/Egyptian community organisation** — outreach angle: request a firsthand speaker on the lived experience of the contemporary uprising, giving students an unmediated voice rather than another historian's lens.

## Place-Based Learning
- **Speaker's Corner, Hyde Park** (Week 1) — students observe live contested-rights rhetoric in action; 5-minute walk from Marble Arch; no booking needed, low logistics.
- **British Museum, Enlightenment Gallery** (Week 4) — students examine Revolutionary-era objects and interrogate inclusion/exclusion in the collection, guided by the curator visit; ~20-minute walk from Marble Arch, free entry; book the curator session at least 4 weeks in advance.

## Differentiation
**Extension:** Students ready for greater complexity can add a fourth comparative case (e.g. the Haitian Revolution's influence on later Latin American independence movements) to their exhibition section, requiring them to trace causality across a longer chain of events rather than three isolated cases.

**Support:** Provide a simplified bias-tracking template with sentence starters ("This source likely favours ___ because ___") for the Source Bias Tracker, and offer a visual timeline scaffold for the Comparative Governance Chart so students can anchor abstract governance concepts to concrete dates and events.

**EAL/Language:** Pre-teach key vocabulary (governance, exclusion, universal rights, perspective, primary source) with visual glossaries before Week 1. Allow exhibition labels to be drafted first in students' first language and translated collaboratively, preserving the sophistication of their thinking rather than limiting it to their English proficiency.

## Framework Alignment
**Transcend 6 Leaps:** Strong on **Agency** (students choose the contemporary case, pitch their own argument), **Connection & Community** (curator, community organisation, public audience), and **Relevance** (Speaker's Corner ties historical rights rhetoric to a living London space). **High Expectations with Rigorous Learning** is delivered through the Criterion D source-analysis work. **Customization** is present but lighter — pacing and product format are fairly fixed; consider offering format choice (physical vs. digital exhibition) in a future iteration.

**PBL Gold Standard:** All 7 elements present. **Challenging Problem/Question**, **Sustained Inquiry**, **Authenticity**, **Public Product**, **Critique & Revision**, and **Reflection** are all strongly built in. **Student Voice & Choice** is present in the contemporary case selection but is the lightest-served element — students have less say over the exhibition's format or curatorial approach than over content choice.
